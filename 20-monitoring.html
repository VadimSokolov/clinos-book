22  Clinical Monitoring

A clinical trial is not self-executing. The protocol may be brilliant, the sites carefully selected, the data capture systems impeccably designed, and the trial can still drift away from its intended course. Good Clinical Practice requires sponsors to “implement a system to manage quality throughout all stages of the trial process” (International Council for Harmonisation 2016), and clinical monitoring is a central component of that system: the systematic oversight that keeps a trial’s conduct safe, competent, and compliant with GCP.

Monitoring protects research participants by identifying safety issues, protocol violations, and inadequate consent processes. It protects data integrity by identifying errors, inconsistencies, and potential fraud. It also protects the sponsor’s investment: a problem caught early can still be corrected, while the same problem caught late may already have contaminated the data or triggered regulatory action.

The person doing this is the monitor, formally the Clinical Research Associate (CRA), the sponsor’s representative at the investigator site. CRAs are typically experienced professionals with clinical or scientific backgrounds, trained in GCP, the protocol’s requirements, and monitoring procedures. A single CRA may be responsible for several sites, visiting each on a regular schedule.

22.1 Types of Monitoring Visits

Four kinds of visit punctuate a site’s life in the trial (Table 22.1).

A site that looks promising on paper but lacks the caseload or infrastructure to deliver is far cheaper to identify before activation than after. That is the work of the Pre-Study Qualification Visit, or site selection visit, which takes place while the sponsor is still deciding where to place the trial. The monitor assesses the site’s interest and its access to the target patient population, the qualifications and availability of the investigator and staff, and whether the facilities and equipment can support the protocol’s procedures.

The Site Initiation Visit (SIV) comes before the site enrolls its first patient. The monitor confirms that regulatory approvals are in place, that staff are trained on the protocol and study procedures, that investigational product has arrived and is properly stored, and that the essential documents are in the Trial Master File. It is also where the working relationship between monitor and site is established.

Interim Monitoring Visits (IMVs) recur throughout the trial, monthly, quarterly, or at whatever interval the monitoring plan sets. During IMVs the monitor reviews enrollment, verifies source data, checks protocol compliance, reviews adverse event documentation, reconciles investigational product inventory, and addresses whatever has gone wrong since the last visit.

The Closeout Visit follows the end of enrollment and follow-up at a site. Data queries must be resolved, investigational product accounted for (returned, destroyed, or documented as dispensed), and site files completed and ready for long-term retention.

22.2 On-Site Monitoring Activities

Source Data Verification (SDV) is the core of an on-site visit: the monitor compares data entered in the trial database against the original source documents (medical records, lab reports, imaging results) to confirm that the two agree. Historical standards often demanded 100% verification; risk-based approaches now aim SDV at the most critical data points. Source Document Review (SDR) asks a broader question of the same records: are they complete, are they consistent, and do they suggest faithful adherence to the protocol?

The monitor must also verify that every participant gave valid informed consent, on the correct approved version of the form, before any study procedure began. Investigational product accountability reconciles dispensing records against physical inventory and storage conditions against the protocol. The Investigator Site File (ISF) has to be current and every staff member’s qualifications documented. The part least amenable to a checklist is conversation: the monitor talks to the site staff, gauging team capability, surfacing training needs, and catching operational bottlenecks before they escalate into systemic issues.

22.3 The Monitoring Report

After each visit the monitor prepares a monitoring report documenting findings, follow-up items, and any corrective actions required. It becomes part of the Trial Master File and stands as the contemporaneous record of what the site looked like on that day.

Monitoring reports should be objective, factual, and specific. “Informed consent was adequate” tells a later reader nothing. “100% of consents reviewed (n=15); all documented appropriately; 2 consents were signed on the same day as randomization (acceptable per protocol)” tells them what was examined, what was found, and why it passed.

Follow-up items are tracked until resolution. A site that is not properly documenting adverse events gets the finding recorded, the corrective action specified, and the fix verified at the subsequent visit.

22.4 Risk-Based Quality Management (RBQM)

A 2025 report by the Association of Clinical Research Organizations (ACRO) found that 96% of the 3,758 outsourced studies reported by seven CROs for 2024 incorporated at least one RBM or RBQM component, such as centralized statistical monitoring (CSM) or targeted SDV, up from 53% in 2019 (Association of Clinical Research Organizations 2025). Traditional monitoring, built on frequent on-site visits and 100% SDV, is now an outdated model. Risk-Based Quality Management (RBQM), called for by ICH E6(R2) and reinforced in E6(R3), focuses oversight on the risks that matter most to patient safety and data reliability (Figure 22.1).

The gains are in detection and in economics. Statistical algorithms can find fabricated data, such as “perfect” blood pressure readings, that a human reviewer would miss; monitors visit sites less frequently, going where the central signals point them; and the budget that once bought travel buys data analytics instead. None of this is less monitoring. It is smarter monitoring, and technology enables oversight that would be impossible through on-site visits alone.

RBM starts with risk assessment: which aspects of this particular trial pose the greatest risk to patient safety or data integrity. A narrow safety margin may pull resources toward safety monitoring, subjective patient-reported endpoints toward endpoint verification, a therapeutic area with a history of data integrity problems toward detecting potential fraud. The resulting risk-based monitoring plan may send low-risk data to remote review or reduced SDV; high-risk data receives more intensive oversight.

Risk-based practice has settled on two named instruments, only the first of which is explicit in the ICH text (International Council for Harmonisation 2016; U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2013). Quality Tolerance Limits (QTLs) are pre-specified thresholds on parameters critical to participant safety and data reliability, set at the protocol level (for example, an acceptable ceiling on the rate of a key eligibility deviation across the whole trial). A breach does not by itself indicate misconduct; it triggers evaluation of whether a systematic problem is present. Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) are site-level metrics (enrollment pace, query rates, adverse-event reporting frequency, protocol-deviation counts) tracked centrally to flag sites whose patterns diverge from expectations and warrant closer scrutiny.

Centralized monitoring complements on-site visits, analyzing trial data for patterns suggestive of problems: unusual distributions, unexpected correlations, digit preferences that suggest data fabrication, enrollment patterns inconsistent with site capacity.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated adoption of remote monitoring, conducted through remote access to electronic health records, video calls with site staff, and remote review of scanned source documents. Some work still requires a physical presence: a video link is a poor substitute for checking pharmacy storage conditions or seeing how a site actually operates. Many other monitoring functions can be performed effectively without a site visit, which is how hybrid monitoring settled into its present shape: periodic on-site visits wrapped in more frequent remote oversight.

22.5 Quality Metrics and Ongoing Oversight

Monitoring sits inside a larger quality system whose three parts are easy to confuse (Figure 22.2).

Quality Control is the day-to-day operational work that builds quality into the trial: training, SOPs, source data verification, template development. Quality Assurance is the overarching system meant to keep that work reliable. Audits are how QA finds out whether that work is achieving its intended outcomes, conducted separately from line operations so that the people judging QC are not the people doing it.

The metrics that indicate site and trial quality are, in risk-based terms, the KRIs described earlier. They work when reviewed regularly and compared across sites, which is how patterns and outliers become visible. A site that consistently under-enrolls may require intervention or closure. A high protocol deviation rate suggests a training need, or a protocol too complex to follow.

Query rates point at data entry problems: a site generating many more queries than its comparators may have inadequate training or inadequate attention to data quality. Adverse event reporting patterns can identify sites over- or under-reporting relative to others, and both directions warrant investigation. Time to issue resolution indicates site responsiveness. A site that leaves queries unresolved or corrective action items unaddressed may need more intensive oversight.

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)

SOPs are written instructions intended to achieve uniformity in the performance of specific functions. One common way to organize the ones a full clinical research operation needs is in two families. Administrative and general SOPs govern the operational framework: the “SOP on SOPs” that defines how procedures are written and approved, along with training documentation, records management (including archiving and destruction), and crisis management plans. Regulatory SOPs cover the ground where a lapse becomes a compliance failure: obtaining informed consent, communicating with the IRB, handling safety reports, managing investigational product from receipt to destruction, and screening and enrolling subjects to ensure eligibility. Section 20.3.2 sets these procedures beside the protocol and follows an SOP from drafting to retirement.

22.6 Audits and Inspections

Monitoring is the sponsor’s own line oversight. Audits and inspections are checks from outside the operating team, and the two differ enough in authority and consequence that conflating them leaves a team under-prepared for the one carrying real regulatory risk.

Sponsor audits are systematic, internal quality assurance activities conducted by the sponsor’s QA group or by independent contractors. Where monitoring is continuous, audits land at strategic intervals: early in the trial to verify initial setup, mid-trial to assess ongoing compliance, or near closeout to confirm inspection readiness. They compare what the site and the sponsor actually did against the protocol, the SOPs, and GCP.

Regulatory inspections are conducted by government authorities such as the FDA, EU national inspectorates (with EMA coordinating those for centralized applications), or Japan’s PMDA, and the stakes are different in kind. An inspection may be routine surveillance, for-cause and triggered by a specific concern, or pre-approval, conducted before a new drug is cleared for market. Findings can produce warning letters, data rejection, or a delayed marketing approval.

Preparing for Inspection

Inspection readiness is a standing operational condition: on the day an inspector arrives, a team either has it or does not. Keeping it means that the TMF and ISF stay current, documents version-controlled and signed; that site and sponsor staff understand their roles and the GCP requirements well enough to answer questions clearly and factually; that retrieval systems can surface a requested record within hours; and that audit trails attribute and explain every data modification. Unexplained deletions and late entries in an audit trail are immediate red flags to any regulatory inspector.

TipChecklist: Digital Inspection Readiness (Computerized Systems)

Inspection readiness now extends to the computerized systems used for trial conduct and data handling. Expectations for electronic systems emphasize validation commensurate with intended use, reliable audit trails, appropriate access controls, and the ability to reconstruct trial-relevant records and signatures (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023, 2024; International Council for Harmonisation 2025).

  • System inventory: list all computerized systems used for trial conduct and data (EDC, ePRO/eCOA, IRT/RTSM, eTMF, QMS, safety database, device platforms), including vendor responsibilities and interfaces (International Council for Harmonisation 2025).
  • Validation and intended use: maintain documentation of validation/qualification aligned to intended use, including change control evidence for material releases (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2024).
  • Audit trails: ensure audit trails are enabled, reviewable, and practically reviewable under inspection timelines; define when routine review is expected (e.g., consent workflows, key efficacy/safety data, late changes) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2024).
  • Access control and identity: role-based access, unique user identities, and controlled electronic signature processes (including account provisioning and deprovisioning) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023, 2024).
  • Record retrieval drills: demonstrate rapid retrieval of critical records (consent documentation, safety reporting, protocol deviation documentation, monitoring reports, essential documents) (International Council for Harmonisation 2025).
  • Data transfer traceability: maintain transfer specifications and reconciliation evidence for transfers between systems, including vendor-managed pipelines (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2024).

During the Inspection

The sponsor typically designates a back room team to support document retrieval and a front room presence to escort inspectors and manage interactions. Daily close-out meetings between inspectors and the sponsor allow clarification of findings before they become formal observations.

Inspectors may request additional documents, interview staff, or tour facilities. Answers should be factual and concise; speculation and defensiveness rarely help. A question that cannot be answered on the spot is better met with a commitment to follow up than with a guess.

Inspection Findings and Responses

Findings are typically graded by severity, and the grade shapes what happens next (Table 22.2).

In the United States, FDA inspectors who observe conditions that may violate the regulations list them on a Form 483, issued at the close of the inspection. The inspected sponsor or investigator may respond in writing within 15 business days, typically with a Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) plan (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2021, 2020), which should address both the immediate finding and the systemic cause that produced it.

Findings tend to cluster around a few constant issues. Informed consent produces a family of them: outdated versions, signatures obtained after procedures have begun, signatures missing altogether. Protocol deviations become findings when they were neither documented nor reported through the appropriate channels, data integrity findings when the trial database and the original medical records disagree, investigational product findings when dispensing, returns, or reconciliation were inadequately documented. On the sponsor’s side, the recurring finding is oversight itself: monitoring activities inadequately documented.

22.7 Trial Governance Committees

Clinical trials, and large Phase III studies especially, also run under specialized governance committees supplying independent expertise and accountability.

Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB)

The Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB; see Section 9.6 for alternate names) provides independent oversight of accumulating safety and efficacy data. It sees unblinded comparative results that are normally withheld from the sponsor and the investigators, which is what allows it to recommend stopping a trial early for safety, futility, or overwhelming efficacy.

Composition typically runs to 3-7 members: clinical experts in the disease area, a biostatistician experienced in interim analyses, and sometimes an ethicist or patient representative. Members must be independent, free from conflicts of interest with the sponsor and uninvolved in the trial’s conduct.

The DSMB operates under a charter that settles in advance what it will examine and what it may do: scope of review (safety only, or safety and efficacy), meeting frequency and the triggers for ad hoc meetings, statistical stopping rules and decision thresholds, the process for communicating recommendations to the sponsor, and confidentiality requirements.

DSMB meetings typically include an open session (where the sponsor presents trial status without unblinding) and a closed session (where the unblinded statistician presents interim analyses to DSMB members only). The DSMB may recommend: continue as planned, modify the protocol, stop enrollment, stop treatment, or stop the trial entirely. Recommendations go to the sponsor, who retains authority for final decisions but must document the rationale if departing from DSMB advice.

NoteNote: Committee Documentation as an Inspection Artifact

Committee governance is a documentation problem: committees matter operationally only insofar as their processes are specified, followed, and evidenced. Meinert’s emphasis on formal committee structures and procedures remains relevant even as meeting modalities shift (e.g., secure virtual or hybrid meetings) (Meinert 2013). From a GCP and inspection standpoint, the relevant artifacts include charters, membership independence and conflict management, meeting minutes, recommendation letters, and controlled distribution of any unblinded information (International Council for Harmonisation 2025).

DSMBs for Platform and Adaptive Trials

A platform DSMB may be overseeing six, eight, or ten treatment arms at once, each at a different stage of enrollment and maturity. The SNAP platform (S. aureus bacteremia) had a single DSMB reviewing multiple treatment domains at once. A DSMB member handed a standard two-column comparison table can form a judgment quickly. The platform equivalent requires holding in mind which arms are still enrolling, which have closed, and which interim result from one domain might change conduct in another.

The board has to be constituted for that complexity. Not every member can be an expert in every arm’s therapy, so domain-specific consultants are sometimes added for particular reviews. Conflicts of interest are harder to manage too: where several companies’ drugs are under evaluation at once, members with no relationship to any sponsor are difficult to find, and disclosure and recusal procedures must be detailed in the charter.

When a single arm fails, the recommendation is arm closure, not trial termination, and the DSMB has to say so precisely: “close domain B for futility in the MSSA population; continue all other domains as specified.” A partial stop of that kind requires a charter that explicitly anticipates arm-level action and specifies how the remaining platform continues without the closed arm. Spillover needs anticipating as well. If domain A’s success changes the standard of care and with it the control event rate for domain B, that has to be surfaced.

Complex Bayesian adaptive platforms also require real-time statistical computation for interim analyses that a DSMB’s own biostatistician may not be equipped to run independently. The standard solution is a separate unblinded statistical team (sometimes called the independent statistical center) that prepares the interim analysis package for the DSMB. This team is firewalled from the sponsor’s statisticians and has full access to unblinded data. The charter must specify exactly what it prepares, what forms of results reach the DSMB (for example, which summaries the sponsor may see in the open session, and in what aggregated form), and how results are archived to support audit.

Adjusting for temporal trends, to account for changes in background treatment or disease severity over the trial’s lifespan, creates a presentation problem of its own: should the DSMB see raw observed data or model-adjusted comparisons? Raw safety data from different time periods may not be directly comparable without the adjustment, and DSMB members without statistical modeling backgrounds may struggle to evaluate model-adjusted estimates. For safety reviews in particular, where model-adjusted data may require machinery that is not readily available, a DSMB may see raw event counts alongside model-adjusted efficacy summaries. The unblinded statistician should be prepared to explain what the model is doing and what a DSMB member would see in the raw data, so the board can calibrate its judgment.

The COVID anticoagulation multi-platform RCT, pooling REMAP-CAP, ATTACC, and ACTIV-4a, used a novel governance structure: each platform kept its own DSMB, but all three reviewed the same combined analyses, produced under a common statistical analysis plan, and coordinated their recommendations. Each platform could then satisfy its own regulatory obligations (each had its own sponsor, protocol, and regulatory and ethics approvals) while the adaptive stopping decisions were made on the full combined evidence. The coordination required detailed pre-specification of how stopping decisions would be communicated across platforms.

Endpoint Adjudication Committees

Where endpoints are subject to interpretation (cardiovascular events, disease progression, cause of death), a Clinical Events Committee (CEC) or Endpoint Adjudication Committee provides independent, blinded review. Adjudication is what keeps endpoint determinations consistent across sites and investigators: a myocardial infarction diagnosed at one hospital should meet the same criteria as one diagnosed at another. The CEC reviews source documents (ECGs, troponin values, imaging) and applies protocol-defined criteria to classify events.

Operationally, adjudication adds timeline pressure. Cases must be prepared, reviewed, and adjudicated before database lock, and large trials with thousands of potential events may require dedicated adjudication infrastructure and close coordination with data management.

Steering Committees

A Steering Committee (SC) provides scientific oversight and guidance on protocol interpretation, drawing its membership from the principal investigators, sponsor representatives, and sometimes independent experts. Its authority is whatever the sponsor delegates to it, from advice alone to primary responsibility for the study’s design and the quality of its conduct (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2006). It may recommend protocol amendments, interpret eligibility questions, and guide publication strategy. It should remain blind to outcomes by treatment arm, and the distinction between scientific guidance (SC) and safety oversight (DSMB) must be maintained to preserve the DSMB’s independence.

1  Historical Context and Evolution

The rules that govern clinical trials were not written in the abstract. They were written after people were harmed. A liquid antibacterial mixed with a toxic solvent killed more than a hundred patients in 1937, many of them children. Nazi physicians ran experiments on concentration camp prisoners without consent. In Tuskegee, researchers watched syphilis progress in Black men for forty years and withheld the cure. Thalidomide left thousands of infants with ruined limbs after their mothers took it for morning sickness. Each failure forced a new protection into law or ethics: safety testing before marketing, voluntary consent, independent review, proof of efficacy. The protocols, monitors, and oversight committees of the modern trial are scar tissue from wounds inflicted on subjects who had no voice.

1.1 The Elixir Sulfanilamide Disaster (1937)

The Elixir Sulfanilamide tragedy of 1937 was the first disaster to demonstrate that commercial drug manufacturing without safety testing could kill on a large scale (U.S. Department of Agriculture 1937). Sulfanilamide was an effective antibacterial drug, but to create a liquid formulation that would appeal to patients who had difficulty swallowing tablets, the S.E. Massengill Company dissolved the drug in diethylene glycol, a sweet-tasting but toxic industrial solvent related to the ethylene glycol used in antifreeze.

The consequences were devastating. The Secretary of Agriculture’s report to Congress traced them to a gap in the law (U.S. Department of Agriculture 1937):

“Before the ‘elixir’ was put on the market, it was tested for flavor but not for its effect on human life. The existing Federal Food and Drugs Act does not require that new drugs be tested before they are placed on sale.”

The report revealed critical failures in the drug development process. The chief chemist testified that no tests were made to determine the toxicity of either the separate ingredients or of the finished product (U.S. Department of Agriculture 1937). The product was labeled simply as “Elixir Sulfanilamide” with no disclosure of diethylene glycol and no warning of danger (U.S. Department of Agriculture 1937). Most of the drug was administered on physicians’ prescriptions. Of the 240 gallons manufactured and distributed, all but about 6 gallons were recovered before they could be consumed; that small quantity was enough to kill, and the report to Congress counted 93 deaths in fifteen states as it was written in late 1937 (U.S. Department of Agriculture 1937). The final toll is generally put at more than 100 people, many of them children (Ballentine 1981).

Existing law did not require proof of safety before marketing. Seizures had to be based on a technicality: the word “elixir” implies an alcoholic solution, whereas this product was a diethylene glycol solution. Had it been called a “solution,” no charge of violating the law could have been brought.

Congress responded to the Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster by passing the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, which for the first time required manufacturers to demonstrate the safety of new drugs before they could be marketed. The 1938 Act left the boundary between physician-prescribed and self-medication drugs unsettled; the Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951 fixed that line in statute, defining which drugs require a practitioner’s supervision and may be dispensed only on prescription (United States Congress 1951).

ImportantKey Regulatory Outcome

The 1938 Act established the principle that new drugs must be proven safe through adequate testing before being marketed: a foundation of modern pharmaceutical regulation.

1.2 The Nuremberg Code (1947)

The atrocities committed by Nazi physicians during World War II represented the darkest chapter in the history of human experimentation. Doctors conducted horrific experiments on concentration camp prisoners without consent, causing immense suffering and death in the name of “medical science.”

After the war, a U.S. military tribunal put 23 German physicians and administrators in the dock for war crimes and crimes against humanity. Before naming who was guilty, the judges set down what lawful human research requires. That statement became the Nuremberg Code: ten principles forged in a criminal judgment, not a professional committee (Nuremberg Military Tribunal 1949; University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill 2024). The first principle is still the one that matters most: no one may be enrolled without free, informed consent. The rest follow from it. The study must be worth doing, the risks must not outrun the possible gain, the investigators must be competent, and the subject must be free to walk away. If the experiment turns harmful, the physician in charge must stop it.

Note

The Nuremberg Code’s emphasis on voluntary consent established the principle that remains central to clinical research ethics today: research subjects must never be coerced and must provide informed consent.

1.3 The Thalidomide Tragedy (1961-1962)

Just over a decade after the Nuremberg Code, another tragedy would reshape pharmaceutical regulation. Thalidomide, a sedative marketed in Europe for sleep and nausea (including morning sickness during pregnancy), caused severe birth defects, primarily phocomelia (limb malformations), in an estimated 10,000 infants worldwide.

In the United States, FDA medical officer Dr. Frances Kelsey refused to approve thalidomide for marketing, citing insufficient safety data. Her diligence prevented widespread tragedy in America, with only 17 cases reported from investigational use.

WarningRegulatory Gap Exposed

Thalidomide revealed that proving safety alone was insufficient: drugs also needed to demonstrate efficacy. The tragedy also exposed the absence of systematic requirements for studying drug effects during pregnancy and for reporting adverse events.

The 1962 amendments required manufacturers to prove both safety and efficacy before marketing. Efficacy had to rest on substantial evidence from adequate and well-controlled investigations: the statutory phrase that still anchors modern Phase III practice (Chapter 9). Clinical pharmacologist Louis Lasagna was among those who pressed Congress for that standard, testifying that adequately controlled drug comparisons were almost impossible to find under the old regime (Junod 2008). The Amendments also required that clinical work be grounded in prior animal studies, established formal Investigational New Drug (IND) applications, and mandated adverse-reaction reporting and informed consent from research subjects.

1.4 The Declaration of Helsinki (1964)

In 1964, the World Medical Association (WMA) adopted the Declaration of Helsinki, which built upon the Nuremberg Code to provide more detailed ethical guidance specifically for physicians conducting medical research. Unlike the Nuremberg Code, which emerged from criminal proceedings, the Declaration was developed by the medical profession itself as a statement of ethical principles (World Medical Association 2013).

The 1964 text built directly on Nuremberg: it distinguished therapeutic from non-therapeutic research, required that studies rest on adequately performed laboratory and animal work and be conducted by scientifically qualified persons, and sharpened the proportionality requirement (research risk must be outweighed by the scientific importance of the objective) from a vague Nuremberg principle into a more operationalized standard. Several protections now regarded as central to the Declaration were added in later revisions rather than in the original: independent committee review of protocols before enrollment entered with the 1975 Tokyo revision (the conceptual ancestor of today’s ethics committees and IRBs), and explicit obligations around trial registration and the publication of results, including negative findings, were strengthened in the revisions of the 2000s.

The Declaration has been revised multiple times, most recently in 2024, to address emerging ethical issues in clinical research (World Medical Association 2025).

1.5 The Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932-1972)

While regulatory frameworks were evolving for pharmaceutical products, ethical failures in government-sponsored research would expose another critical gap. Between 1932 and 1972, the U.S. Public Health Service conducted the Tuskegee Syphilis Study on approximately 600 low-income African-American men in rural Alabama (399 with syphilis and 201 controls without) to observe the natural progression of untreated syphilis.

Ethical violations were systemic: participants were misled into believing they were being treated for “bad blood,” a local catch-all that obscured syphilis, and after penicillin became standard therapy in the mid-1940s researchers both withheld it and blocked men from getting treatment elsewhere until the study ended in 1972.

The study’s exposure in 1972 forced major reforms in research ethics and the establishment of systematic oversight mechanisms.

1.6 The Belmont Report (1979)

In response to the Tuskegee study and other ethical violations, the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research produced the Belmont Report in 1979 (National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research 1979). They became binding federal rules through the Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects—the Common Rule—adopted in 1991 and codified for HHS (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2024). The Common Rule requires prior IRB review and approval, documented informed consent, and additional protections for pregnant women, prisoners, and children; at adoption it aligned those requirements across fifteen federal departments and agencies. For clinical investigations of drugs and devices, FDA imposes parallel obligations for informed consent and IRB review (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023a, 2023b). The 2018 revision, among other changes, generally requires a single IRB for U.S. multi-site research funded by Common Rule agencies and a concise “key information” section at the start of consent forms. How IRBs exercise that authority in practice is covered in Chapter 4. Internationally, the Declaration of Helsinki and the CIOMS guidelines fill the corresponding ethical role.

1.7 ICH and Global Harmonization (1990s-Present)

As pharmaceutical development became increasingly global, the need for harmonized standards became clear. The International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) was established in 1990 to bring together regulatory authorities and pharmaceutical industries from Europe, Japan, and the United States.

ICH E6 GCP: Evolution

The ICH Guideline for Good Clinical Practice (E6) was first adopted in 1996 and established an international ethical and scientific quality standard for designing, conducting, recording, and reporting clinical trials (International Council for Harmonisation 1996). The guideline has evolved significantly through subsequent revisions: E6(R2) in 2016 introduced risk-based monitoring and quality management systems (International Council for Harmonisation 2016), while E6(R3) in 2025 restructured GCP into a Principles document with Annexes, introducing Quality by Design, risk-proportionate oversight, decentralized trial guidance, and technology-neutral data standards (International Council for Harmonisation 2025b). This evolution reflects how GCP principles have adapted to increasingly complex and technology-enabled trial designs.

NoteICH E6(R3) Implementation

ICH E6(R3) Principles and Annex 1 became effective in the EU on July 23, 2025 (European Medicines Agency 2024). Annex 2, which addresses decentralized, pragmatic, and other non-traditional interventional trials, reached Step 4 in June 2026 and takes effect in the EU on January 15, 2027.

The GCP Principles

GCP rests on ethical foundations from the Declaration of Helsinki. The core requirement is that subject welfare takes priority over scientific objectives. Before trials begin, sponsors must demonstrate that preclinical data justify human testing and that risks are reasonable given expected benefits. Trials require clear protocols, qualified investigators, physician oversight of medical decisions, and adherence to approved procedures throughout.

Additional principles cover consent documentation, data integrity, participant confidentiality, drug manufacturing standards, and quality management systems.

1.8 Recent Developments (2020s)

The 2020s pushed clinical trials toward a more technology-enabled, participant-centered, and globally harmonized operating model. COVID-19 forced sponsors and sites to redesign “on the fly” for continuity (remote visits, alternative safety labs, local care, shipment logistics, and documentation of protocol deviations), and FDA codified many of these practices in its guidance on trial conduct during the public health emergency (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2021). Those contingencies then hardened into durable operating patterns. FDA finalized guidance on digital health technologies for remote data acquisition in 2023, stressing verification, validation, data integrity, and operational controls that make remotely collected endpoints usable for regulatory decisions (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023d). In 2024 it followed with a framework for decentralized elements—telehealth and in-home visits, local healthcare providers, direct-to-participant investigational product shipment, and electronic consent—treating decentralization as a set of designable elements rather than an all-or-nothing trial type (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2024c).

Methodological change was equally significant. Adaptive and simulation-driven designs, long used in selected programs, moved toward routine options for efficiency and decision quality. Expectations for pre-specified adaptation rules, trial integrity protections, and simulation of operating characteristics had already been set out in FDA’s 2019 adaptive design guidance (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2019); the 2020s added broader uptake and international harmonization through the draft ICH E20 guideline (Step 2b, endorsed June 2025), which proposes common vocabulary and best practices, including simulation, multiplicity control, and operational-bias mitigation (International Council for Harmonisation 2025a).

Bayesian methods received particular regulatory attention. Building on principles FDA had already applied to medical devices (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2010), the agency’s January 2026 draft guidance for drugs and biologics treats Bayesian approaches as appropriate for primary inference, including in trials that borrow external information (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026). The guidance specifies what sponsors must demonstrate:

  • pre-specification of Bayesian success criteria
  • evaluation of operating characteristics through simulation
  • careful justification of prior distributions (especially when borrowing)
  • planned sensitivity analyses for prior-data conflict
  • documentation sufficient for regulatory review and reproducibility

This framework converts “Bayesian acceptability” from an abstract question into a set of reviewable components: decision rules, error control, effective sample size calculations, and computational reproducibility. The practical result is that sponsors increasingly treat trial design as an iterative engineering exercise, stress-testing decision thresholds across realistic scenarios before the first patient is enrolled.

A third shift has been representation and equity, which the 2022 FDORA statute elevated from aspirational rhetoric to a statutory requirement. FDA’s draft guidance on Diversity Action Plans, issued under FDORA, describes when plans are required, what they must contain, how waivers are evaluated, and how sponsors should translate enrollment goals into actionable recruitment and retention strategies (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2024a). The regulatory framing has since become contested. FDA removed the draft Diversity Action Plans guidance from its website after the January 2025 executive order on diversity programs, restored it weeks later under court order, and let the statutory deadline for the final Diversity Action Plans guidance pass without issuing it (Grossi 2025). A separate document, FDA’s December 2025 final guidance on trial participation (a revision of its long-standing eligibility-criteria guidance, issued under a different statutory mandate), retains the expectation of representative enrollment while dropping the ‘diversity’ framing (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2025). At the same time, the growing use of master protocols and platform approaches, accelerated by COVID-era experience, has been accompanied by regulatory efforts to standardize expectations around governance, control of multiplicity, and interpretability across continuously evolving trial infrastructures (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023e).

Automation became a structural response to scale and complexity. Risk-based quality management (RBQM) and centralized monitoring matured into analytics-first oversight models, supported by statistical anomaly detection and machine learning to prioritize site follow-up, target source data verification, and detect data quality and compliance risks earlier (Association of Clinical Research Organizations 2025). Across the clinical operations stack, AI/ML is being applied to protocol authoring and feasibility, patient-to-trial matching, safety case triage, and Trial Master File processing; the market growth of AI tooling in clinical trials reflects that these capabilities are moving from pilot projects into production workflows (Fortune Business Insights 2024). This trend does not remove the need for human accountability. It shifts effort toward governance, validation, and auditability so automated decisions can be explained and defended.

AI/ML has also reshaped evidence generation, not just operations. The availability of large-scale electronic health record (EHR) and claims datasets made externally controlled analyses and hybrid designs more common, but only when the underlying data are demonstrably “fit for purpose.” FDA’s 2023 guidance on using real-world data (RWD) and real-world evidence (RWE) lays out how sponsors should justify data relevance, reliability, and analytic validity for regulatory decisions (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023c). In 2024, FDA finalized more prescriptive guidance on assessing EHR and medical claims sources for regulatory-grade use, reflecting a shift from “RWE is promising” to “RWE is acceptable when you can prove provenance, completeness, linkage quality, and outcome ascertainment” (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2024b). Practically, this opens the door to more credible external controls, contextualization of single-arm studies, and post-market effectiveness/safety questions, while raising the bar for data engineering, bias assessment, and reproducibility.

A further transition is emerging: from automation as a feature to automation as an operating model. The concept of “agentic AI” (systems that can plan, execute, and verify multi-step workflows rather than simply generate text) is beginning to appear in clinical operations, though governance lags behind capability. The regulatory question is not whether AI can draft a protocol section or flag a safety signal, but whether it can do so in a way that preserves data integrity, produces audit-ready traces, and keeps humans accountable for decisions that affect participant safety. Standards bodies and regulators are adapting: NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework (2023) provides a structure for identifying hazards and implementing controls (National Institute of Standards and Technology 2023), while earlier human-AI interaction guidelines still emphasize making uncertainty visible and supporting oversight (Amershi et al. 2019). The practical implication is that sponsors deploying AI must now budget for governance (validation, monitoring, and documentation) just as they budget for the software itself.

In Europe, the 2020s turned the Clinical Trials Regulation (adopted in 2014) into working infrastructure. The Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) became the single submission and coordinated-assessment channel for multinational trials, with a public transparency layer; it went live on January 31, 2022, became mandatory for new applications on January 31, 2023, and completed transition of legacy trials by January 31, 2025 (European Medicines Agency 2022).

Remote visits, CTIS portals, and model-assisted monitoring change how trials run. They do not change what the history in this chapter was for: keeping subjects from being harmed so that someone else can finish a study.
